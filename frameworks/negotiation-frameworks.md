# Core Negotiation Frameworks

These are the foundations of the Negotiation War Room Skill. Select and combine relevant frameworks before creating strategy; do more than draft wording.

## 1. BATNA

### Definition

Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement: the best actionable alternative if talks fail. It determines whether the user can take a firm position and how far that position can go.

### When to use it

- Client discounts, compensation, contracts, procurement and sales, partnerships.
- Questions such as "Should I concede?", "Can I refuse?", or "Should I keep negotiating?"
- Threats to change suppliers, withdraw an offer, delay a balance, or terminate cooperation.

### Method

1. List actionable alternatives to agreement.
2. Assess their benefits, costs, time, and risks.
3. Select the most realistic, executable alternative.
4. Compare every proposal with it; do not lightly accept less.
5. If it is weak, build bargaining assets and evidence, gain time, or reduce confrontation first.

### Common mistakes

- Treating a hoped-for good outcome as a BATNA.
- Using an unworkable alternative to justify firm wording.
- Revealing the BATNA too early and exposing bargaining assets.
- Considering only money and ignoring time, reputation, relationships, and execution costs.

## 2. ZOPA

### Definition

Zone of Possible Agreement: the range in which both sides do better than their BATNAs.

### When to use it

- Quantifiable issues: price, salary, outstanding balances, compensation, revenue sharing, payment periods and milestones.
- Scope, delivery standards, and responsibility boundaries.
- Both sides have expressed conditions but the agreement range remains unclear.

### Method

1. Identify the user's walk-away limit and BATNA.
2. Estimate the other party's limit and BATNA.
3. Find overlap across price, time, scope, payment, responsibility, resources, and other variables.
4. Without overlap on one variable, add others: scope for price, payment for discount, duration for resources.
5. If unclear, probe before committing to figures.

### Common mistakes

- Treating the ZOPA as only a price range.
- Quoting the minimum before assessing the other party's BATNA.
- Assuming no price overlap means no deal, overlooking scope, time, and risk.
- Accepting terms worse than one's BATNA merely to close.

## 3. Anchoring

### Definition

Use opening terms or reference standards to shape perceptions of a reasonable range. Anchors can concern price, salary, payment percentages, scope, industry standards, or past results.

### When to use it

- Quotes, compensation, damages, revenue shares, discounts, purchasing prices.
- Extreme opening demands such as "30% off" or "this is all we can offer."
- Restoring a reasonable range after a low anchor.

### Method

1. Prepare objective references: market or historical prices, costs, scope, industry standards, performance data.
2. When opening, anchor above the acceptable target without departing from facts.
3. After a low anchor, reset the evaluation criteria before discussing terms.
4. Tie anchors to scope, risk, and execution quality rather than price alone.

### Common mistakes

- Unsupported extreme figures that undermine credibility.
- Negotiating around the other party's low number.
- Revealing the true limit early.
- Aggressive commercial anchoring in sensitive relationships.

## 4. Interest-based negotiation

### Definition

Examine why the other party wants a condition, rather than responding only to their stated position. Positions are demands; interests are the reasons behind them.

### When to use it

- Discounts, extensions, refunds, raises, compensation, or changed terms.
- Work with friends, family relationships, and partnerships.
- An impasse built around reciprocal demands for concessions.

### Method

1. Restate the request to reduce confrontation.
2. Ask about constraints: budget, approvals, risk, time, KPIs, dignity, relationships.
3. Separate core, secondary, and emotional interests from risk terms.
4. Exchange secondary interests while protecting core interests.
5. State the shared goal, such as continued cooperation with clear execution.

### Common mistakes

- Assuming understanding obliges agreement.
- Discussing feelings without execution conditions.
- Stating principles without designing concrete exchanges.
- Ignoring the user's limits and BATNA.

## 5. MESO

### Definition

Multiple Equivalent Simultaneous Offers: several offers of similar value to the user but with different combinations of terms, used to discover preferences and improve agreement likelihood.

### When to use it

- Discounts, procurement and sales, business partnerships, contracts, compensation.
- Uncertainty about whether the other party values price, time, scope, risk, or relationships most.
- A single offer invites repeated price pressure.

### Method

1. Establish the user's limit and core interests.
2. Design two or three genuinely acceptable offers.
3. Vary price, scope, payment, duration, risk, and resources.
4. Ask about preferences rather than only acceptance.
5. Infer underlying interests from those preferences.

### Common mistakes

- Three prices for the same terms.
- An unacceptable decoy option.
- Benefits to the other party with no definite return.
- Missing dates, responsibilities, and acceptance criteria.

## 6. Trade-offs

### Definition

Tie concessions to returns. The user can concede secondary interests in exchange for payment, commitments, scope, time, risk sharing, resources, or a written record.

### When to use it

- Discounts, extensions, free support, extra deliverables, changed payment or liability terms.
- Requests to "do me a favor," "start first and discuss it later," or "make it cheaper this time."
- The user wants to protect a relationship but cannot bear unconditional costs.

### Method

1. Separate core and secondary interests.
2. Concede only secondary interests.
3. Specify what every concession secures.
4. Make progressively smaller concessions, rather than reaching the limit at once.
5. Near the limit, adjust scope, payment, time, and risk instead.

### Common mistakes

- Exchanging a free concession for a vague promise of future cooperation.
- Cutting price without reducing scope.
- Extending time without a new date.
- Accepting risk without a liability cap.

## 7. Loss aversion

### Definition

People often fear losing existing benefits more than they value equivalent gains. Explain the costs of failing to resolve an issue reasonably, without threats or intimidation.

### When to use it

- A client considers changing suppliers despite high switching costs.
- Delayed payment stalls cooperation.
- Ambiguous terms may cause disputes.
- Friends or family overlook damage to a relationship.

### Method

1. Explain the cost of inaction using facts.
2. Emphasize shared losses rather than threatening the other party.
3. Offer an actionable alternative with lower costs.
4. Connect the cost assessment to a next action.

### Common mistakes

- Turning a cost reminder into a threat.
- Exaggerating consequences and losing credibility.
- Creating anxiety without a solution.
- Using commercial pressure tactics in family relationships.

## 8. Documented commitments

### Definition

Turn verbal understanding into clear, traceable, executable commitments. Written records reduce misunderstandings and execution risk.

### When to use it

- Payments, contracts, procurement and sales, partnerships, disputes.
- Confirming the outcome of a call or meeting.
- Repeated delays, reversals, or unfulfilled verbal promises.

### Method

1. Send a confirmation after the discussion.
2. Specify agreed points, responsibilities, amounts, dates, scope, and next steps.
3. Set a deadline for corrections.
4. Add no unconfirmed commitments.
5. For formal legal documents, state that the signed text governs.

### Common mistakes

- Treating verbal promises as a settled agreement.
- Emotional or aggressive confirmation messages.
- Recording only that a discussion happened, without amounts, dates, or responsibilities.
- Admitting liability or waiving rights in a legal dispute without proper consideration.
